2.1 Discovery of Sub-atomic Particles

Discovery of Electron — Cathode Ray Experiment

In 1897, J.J. Thomson discovered the electron using cathode ray discharge tube. When a high voltage (~10,000 V) is applied across a sealed discharge tube at very low pressure (~0.01 mm Hg), invisible rays travel from cathode to anode called cathode rays.

Properties of cathode rays:

  • Travel in straight lines
  • Consist of negatively charged particles (electrons)
  • Can rotate a light paddle wheel — have kinetic energy
  • Deflected by electric and magnetic fields
  • Same charge/mass ratio regardless of the cathode material

Charge to mass ratio (e/m) of electron = 1.758820 × 10¹¹ C/kg (determined by Thomson)

Charge of Electron — Millikan's Oil Drop Experiment

R.A. Millikan (1909) determined the charge on an electron using oil drop experiment:

Charge on electron (e) = 1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

Mass of electron = e / (e/m) = 9.1094 × 10⁻³¹ kg

Discovery of Proton

E. Goldstein (1886) discovered canal rays (anode rays) — positively charged particles. The lightest canal ray particle (from hydrogen) was called proton by Rutherford.

Mass of proton = 1.6726 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (≈ 1836 times mass of electron)

Charge of proton = +1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

Discovery of Neutron

James Chadwick (1932) discovered neutrons by bombarding beryllium with α-particles.

Mass of neutron = 1.6749 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (slightly heavier than proton)

Charge = 0 (neutral particle)

2.2 Atomic Models

Thomson's Model (Plum Pudding Model) — 1898

Atom is a sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded in it like plums in a pudding. Failed to explain results of Rutherford's α-particle scattering experiment.

Rutherford's Nuclear Model — 1911

Rutherford bombarded thin gold foil with α-particles (He²⁺):

  • Most α-particles passed through undeflected → atom is mostly empty space
  • A few were deflected by small angles → positive charge is concentrated
  • Very few (~1 in 20,000) bounced back → dense, positively charged nucleus

Conclusions: Atom has a small, dense, positively charged nucleus. Electrons revolve around it in circular paths. The nucleus contains most of the mass.

Limitations: Could not explain stability of atom (accelerating electron should continuously radiate energy and spiral into nucleus) and line spectra of atoms.

Bohr's Model — 1913

Niels Bohr postulated (for hydrogen-like atoms):

  • Electrons revolve in fixed circular orbits called stationary states or energy levels
  • Each orbit has a fixed energy — E₁ < E₂ < E₃ ...
  • Angular momentum is quantized: mvr = nh/2π
  • Energy is absorbed/emitted only when an electron jumps between orbits: ΔE = E₂ - E₁ = hν

Limitations: Failed for multi-electron atoms. Could not explain Zeeman effect, Stark effect, or fine spectral lines. Did not account for wave nature of electrons.

2.3 Electromagnetic Radiation

Electromagnetic radiation is a form of energy that is transmitted through space as oscillating electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation.

Key parameters:

  • Wavelength (λ) — distance between two consecutive crests or troughs
  • Frequency (ν) — number of waves passing a point per second (Hz)
  • Wave number (ν̄) — number of waves per unit length = 1/λ
  • Speed of light (c) = 3 × 10⁸ m/s; c = νλ

2.4 Planck's Quantum Theory

Max Planck (1900) proposed that energy is absorbed or emitted in discrete packets called quanta (photons for light).

E = hν = hc/λ, where h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (Planck's constant)

Photoelectric Effect

When light of sufficient frequency strikes a metal surface, electrons are ejected. Explained by Einstein (1905):

hν = hν₀ + ½mv² (where ν₀ = threshold frequency, hν₀ = work function)

2.5 Atomic Spectra

Line spectrum of hydrogen — different series:

  • Lyman series: n₂ → n₁ = 1 (UV region)
  • Balmer series: n₂ → n₁ = 2 (Visible region)
  • Paschen series: n₂ → n₁ = 3 (IR region)
  • Brackett series: n₂ → n₁ = 4 (IR region)
  • Pfund series: n₂ → n₁ = 5 (Far IR region)

2.6 Dual Nature of Matter — de Broglie Equation

Louis de Broglie (1924) proposed that matter, like light, has dual (wave-particle) nature:

λ = h/mv = h/p

This is significant only for microscopic particles (electrons, protons) — for macroscopic objects, the wavelength is negligibly small.

2.7 Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

It is impossible to simultaneously determine the exact position and exact momentum of a microscopic particle:

Δx × Δp ≥ h/4π (or Δx × m × Δv ≥ h/4π)

This principle rules out the concept of definite orbits (Bohr model) and introduces the concept of orbitals — regions of probability.

2.8 Quantum Mechanical Model

Based on Schrödinger wave equation (1926): Ĥψ = Eψ

Solutions give wave functions (ψ) — |ψ|² represents probability density of finding the electron.

Quantum Numbers

  • Principal quantum number (n): Shell, energy level. n = 1, 2, 3... Values: K, L, M, N...
  • Azimuthal quantum number (l): Subshell, shape of orbital. l = 0 to (n-1). s(0), p(1), d(2), f(3)
  • Magnetic quantum number (mₗ): Orientation of orbital. mₗ = -l to +l (total 2l+1 values)
  • Spin quantum number (mₛ): Spin of electron. +½ or -½

Shapes of Orbitals

  • s orbitals: Spherical, non-directional. 1 orbital per subshell.
  • p orbitals: Dumbbell-shaped, directional (pₓ, pᵧ, p_z). 3 orbitals per subshell.
  • d orbitals: Double dumbbell/cloverleaf. 5 orbitals per subshell.
  • f orbitals: Complex shape. 7 orbitals per subshell.

2.9 Filling of Orbitals — Electronic Configuration

Aufbau Principle

Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p → 7s → 5f → 6d → 7p

(n+l) rule: Orbitals with lower (n+l) value fill first. If (n+l) is same, orbital with lower n fills first.

Pauli Exclusion Principle

No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers. Each orbital can hold a maximum of 2 electrons with opposite spins.

Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity

Electrons are distributed among orbitals of equal energy in such a way that maximum number of unpaired electrons with same spin is obtained.

Pairing begins only after all degenerate orbitals are singly occupied.

Exceptional Configurations

Cr (Z=24): [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ (not 3d⁴ 4s²) — half-filled d gives extra stability

Cu (Z=29): [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (not 3d⁹ 4s²) — fully-filled d gives extra stability

Q1. Calculate the wavelength associated with an electron moving with velocity 2.05 × 10⁷ m/s. (Mass of electron = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg, h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)
Using de Broglie equation: λ = h/mv
λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴) / (9.1 × 10⁻³¹ × 2.05 × 10⁷)
λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴) / (1.8655 × 10⁻²³)
λ = 3.55 × 10⁻¹¹ m = 0.0355 nm
Q2. Calculate the energy of a photon of radiation with wavelength 300 nm.
E = hc/λ
E = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3 × 10⁸) / (300 × 10⁻⁹)
E = (1.9878 × 10⁻²⁵) / (3 × 10⁻⁷)
E = 6.626 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
Q3. Calculate the radius and energy of the electron in the 3rd orbit of hydrogen atom.
Radius: rₙ = 0.529 × n²/Z Å
r₃ = 0.529 × 9/1 = 4.761 Å

Energy: Eₙ = -13.6 × Z²/n² eV
E₃ = -13.6 × 1/9 = -1.51 eV
Q4. Calculate the wave number of the first line in the Balmer series of hydrogen spectrum. (R∞ = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹)
Balmer series: n₁ = 2, first line: n₂ = 3
1/λ = R∞ (1/n₁² - 1/n₂²)
1/λ = 1.097 × 10⁷ (1/4 - 1/9)
1/λ = 1.097 × 10⁷ × 5/36
1/λ = 1.524 × 10⁶ m⁻¹
λ = 6.56 × 10⁻⁷ m = 656 nm (red light)
Q5. How many spectral lines are produced when an electron in H atom falls from the 5th orbit to the ground state?
Number of spectral lines = n(n-1)/2
Here n = 5 (since it falls from 5th to 1st orbit)
Lines = 5 × 4 / 2 = 10 spectral lines
Q6. Calculate the uncertainty in position of an electron if uncertainty in its velocity is 0.01% of 6 × 10⁵ m/s.
Δv = 0.01/100 × 6 × 10⁵ = 60 m/s
Δx ≥ h / (4π × m × Δv)
Δx ≥ (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴) / (4 × 3.14 × 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ × 60)
Δx ≥ (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴) / (6.858 × 10⁻²⁸)
Δx ≥ 9.66 × 10⁻⁷ m ≈ 966 nm
Students confuse orbits (Bohr's definite circular paths) with orbitals (quantum mechanical probability regions). Orbits are 2D paths; orbitals are 3D probability clouds.
Cr (Z=24) is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ (NOT 3d⁴ 4s²) and Cu (Z=29) is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (NOT 3d⁹ 4s²). Half-filled and fully-filled subshells have extra stability due to symmetry and exchange energy.
The number of radial nodes = n - l - 1 and angular nodes = l. Total nodes = n - 1. Students often mix these up.
In the (n+l) rule, when (n+l) values are the same, the orbital with lower n fills first (e.g., 3d before 4p since both have n+l = 5, but 3d has n=3).
Heisenberg's principle applies to simultaneous measurement of position and momentum. It does NOT mean we can never know position or momentum — just not both precisely at the same time.
The Rydberg formula gives wave number (1/λ), not wavelength directly. Always check if the question asks for wavelength or wave number!

Chapter Summary

  • Electron discovered by J.J. Thomson (cathode rays), proton by Goldstein (canal rays), neutron by Chadwick
  • Thomson's plum pudding model → Rutherford's nuclear model → Bohr's model → Quantum mechanical model
  • Electromagnetic radiation: c = νλ; Energy is quantized: E = hν
  • Photoelectric effect: hν = hν₀ + ½mv² (Einstein)
  • Hydrogen spectrum: Lyman (UV), Balmer (visible), Paschen (IR), Brackett, Pfund
  • de Broglie: λ = h/mv (matter has dual nature)
  • Heisenberg: Δx × Δp ≥ h/4π (cannot know both position and momentum precisely)
  • Quantum numbers: n (shell), l (subshell/shape), mₗ (orientation), mₛ (spin)
  • Orbital shapes: s (spherical), p (dumbbell), d (double dumbbell), f (complex)
  • Filling rules: Aufbau (increasing energy), Pauli (max 2e⁻ per orbital), Hund's (maximize unpaired e⁻)
  • Exceptional: Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹, Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹
